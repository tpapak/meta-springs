From probability to energy to force

The spring analogy begins with the likelihood. For a Gaussian observation, the negative log-likelihood is a quadratic energy, and its restoring force is linear — Hooke’s law. We can apply the same construction to a binomial observation: write its likelihood as an exponential, identify the energy, and differentiate to obtain the force.

For one arm, let $\ell$ be the model coordinate. Define energy as $E(\ell)=-\log L(\ell)$, ignoring terms independent of $\ell$, and the force in the direction of increasing $\ell$ as $F(\ell)=-\mathrm dE/\mathrm d\ell$. The local stiffness is the energy curvature,

$$ \kappa(\ell)=\frac{\mathrm d^2E}{\mathrm d\ell^2} =-\frac{\mathrm dF}{\mathrm d\ell}. $$

Gaussian observation: a linear spring

With observed arm mean $y$ and known sampling variance $v$,

$$ L_G(\ell;y)\propto\exp\!\left[-\frac{(y-\ell)^2}{2v}\right], \qquad E_G(\ell)=\frac{(y-\ell)^2}{2v}. $$

Differentiating gives

$$ F_G(\ell)=-\frac{\mathrm dE_G}{\mathrm d\ell} =\frac{y-\ell}{v}. $$

The force is proportional to displacement from the rest length $y$, with constant stiffness $\kappa_G=1/v$, so $F_G(\ell)=\kappa_G(y-\ell)$.

Binomial observation: a nonlinear spring

For $e$ events among $n$ observations, the binomial probability is

$$ \Pr(e\mid n,p)=\binom ne p^e(1-p)^{n-e}. $$

Use log-odds as the coordinate: $\ell=\operatorname{logit}(p)$, so $p=\sigma(\ell)=1/(1+\exp(-\ell))$. Viewed as a likelihood for $\ell$,

$$ L_B(\ell;e)\propto \exp\!\left[e\ell-n\log(1+\exp(\ell))\right]. $$

The binomial coefficient is independent of $\ell$, so it contributes only an additive constant to the energy. Thus

$$ E_B(\ell)=n\log(1+\exp(\ell))-e\ell, \qquad \boxed{F_B(\ell)=-\frac{\mathrm dE_B}{\mathrm d\ell}=e-n\sigma(\ell).} $$

The force is observed minus expected events: a positive force increases the predicted probability, and a negative force decreases it. The energy gradient has the opposite sign, $E_B'(\ell)=n\sigma(\ell)-e$. Because $\sigma$ is bounded, the force saturates between $e-n$ and $e$. For $0 \lt e \lt n$, it vanishes at the empirical log-odds $\ell^*=\operatorname{logit}(e/n)$. If $e=0$ or $e=n$, an isolated arm has no finite likelihood-maximizing rest length.

The binomial spring’s stiffness varies with its coordinate:

$$ \kappa_B(\ell)=n\sigma(\ell)\bigl(1-\sigma(\ell)\bigr)=np(1-p). $$

This is the binomial Fisher information for the log-odds. It is largest at $p=1/2$ and tends to zero in either tail. The animation labels this same local stiffness $w(\ell)$, the usual IRLS weight.

In the network notation, arm $j$ of study $i$ has coordinate $\ell_{ij}=x^t_j-x^\tau_{ij}$ and energy

$$ E_{ij}(\ell_{ij})=n_{ij}\log(1+\exp(\ell_{ij}))-e_{ij}\ell_{ij}. $$

Solving the nonlinear network

The network no longer solves in one shot. Newton / IRLS relaxes it: linearise each nonlinear spring at its current length into a local Hookean spring of stiffness $\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_B(\hat\ell_{ij})=w_{ij}$, re-solve the now-linear network, and repeat to convergence. At the solution, the local quadratic approximation has variance $v_{ij}=1/\kappa_{ij}=1/w_{ij}$. This is a local curvature equivalence; the full likelihood remains binomial. The next page introduces the heterogeneity springs used alongside these observation springs.

Beyond the binomial: exponential-family springs

The same construction extends to every regular exponential-family likelihood. In its natural coordinate $\ell$, with any dispersion parameter held fixed, write

$$ p(y\mid\ell)=h(y)\exp\!\left[\ell T(y)-A(\ell)\right]. $$

Taking the negative logarithm and differentiating gives

$$ E(\ell)=A(\ell)-\ell T(y),\qquad F(\ell)=T(y)-A'(\ell),\qquad \kappa(\ell)=A''(\ell). $$

Here $T(y)$ is the sufficient statistic and $A$ is the log-normalising function: its first derivative is the expected statistic and its second derivative is its variance. Thus the force is again observed minus expected, and the stiffness is the Fisher information in the natural coordinate (exponential-family identities). For example, a Poisson count with log-rate $\ell$ gives $F=y-\exp(\ell)$ and $\kappa=\exp(\ell)$. With several natural parameters, the derivatives become a gradient and a stiffness matrix; with a noncanonical link, the derivatives must also account for the change of coordinate. The spring construction therefore extends beyond binary data, although each likelihood still needs its own implementation. Currently, the Haskell and Rust solvers implement Gaussian and binomial observation likelihoods; Rust also has a binomial log-link path for risk ratios. Poisson and Gamma observation likelihoods are not currently implemented.

FAQ
Binomial data — exact likelihood or normal approximation? (multinma does exact binomial)

Exact binomial energy. The observation spring carries $n_{ij}\log(1+e^{\ell})-e_{ij}\ell$, and the per-$\tau^2$ solve is a damped-Newton / IRLS relaxation to the joint mode; only the volume factor (the log-det of the Hessian) is Gaussian. It is not a normal approximation of the likelihood or a fixed-variance log-OR. Against multinma’s exact-binomial MCMC, contrast posterior means agree within $\sim0.02$ log-odds and $\tau^2$ medians within Monte-Carlo error; on synthetic $T{=}6$ binary networks with known $\tau^2$, spring-$\Xi$ has about half the $\tau^2$ RMSE.
