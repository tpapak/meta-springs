Conventional heterogeneity, represented by arm springs

We use $\tau^2$ for the between-study variance of a treatment contrast, as in the usual Lu–Ades NMA model. Give each arm an independent deviation

$$ \zeta_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2/2). $$

For treatment b relative to a in study i, the true study-specific effect is

$$ \boxed{\theta_{i,ab}=(d_b-d_a)+(\zeta_{ib}-\zeta_{ia}).} $$

Here $d_b-d_a$ is the population mean contrast; the difference of arm deviations is its study-specific departure. The study baseline cancels. Consequently,

$$ \Var(\theta_{i,ab}\mid d,\tau^2) =\Var(\zeta_{ib})+\Var(\zeta_{ia}) =\frac{\tau^2}{2}+\frac{\tau^2}{2}=\tau^2. $$

For two contrasts sharing reference arm a,

$$ \operatorname{Cov}(\theta_{i,ab},\theta_{i,ac}\mid d,\tau^2) =\Var(\zeta_{ia})=\frac{\tau^2}{2}. $$

Thus their correlation is $1/2$. For all contrasts against one reference in a multi-arm study, the heterogeneity covariance is exactly $\tau^2(I+J)/2$, where $I$ is the identity and $J$ the all-ones matrix. Independent arm variances of $\tau^2/2$ reproduce the standard Lu–Ades contrast model, including its multi-arm dependence. With equivalent baseline treatment and matching priors, this is a reparameterisation: it gives the same contrast likelihood and inference. See also the exchangeability page.

Effective stiffness

Each arm deviation is the length of a $\tau$-spring, with prior stiffness $k_\tau=2/\tau^2$ and mean-square length $\langle\zeta_{ij}^2\rangle=\tau^2/2$. The name refers to the shared heterogeneity parameter; an individual spring’s variance is half of it.

At a given $\tau^2$, the Gaussian network is still linear. The observation spring (variance $v_{ij}$) and heterogeneity spring (variance $\tau^2/2$) combine in series, giving

$$ \boxed{\;\keff_{ij}=\frac{1}{v_{ij}+\tau^2/2}\;} $$

where $v_{ij}=1/w_{ij}$ and the arm weight $w_{ij}$ — the observation-spring hardness — is

$$ w_{ij} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{n_{ij}}{s_{ij}^2}, & \text{Gaussian } \bigl(v_{ij}=s_{ij}^2/n_{ij}\bigr),\\[8pt] n_{ij}\,\sigma(\hat\ell_{ij})\bigl(1-\sigma(\hat\ell_{ij})\bigr), & \text{binomial (Fisher information)}. \end{cases} $$

so equivalently $\keff_{ij} = \dfrac{w_{ij}}{1 + w_{ij}\,\tau^2/2}$. Each $\keff_{ij}$ is one edge of a weighted graph-Laplacian $A(\tau^2)$ on the study and treatment nodes (a non-reference arm adds $+\keff_{ij}$ to the study–study and treatment–treatment diagonals and $-\keff_{ij}$ to the study–treatment off-diagonals, the right-hand side carrying $\keff_{ij}\,y_{ij}$). Solving the single linear system

$$ A(\tau^2)\,x \;=\; b(\tau^2), \qquad x = (\mu_1,\dots,\mu_S,\;d_2,\dots,d_T) $$

places every node; the treatment effects $d_t$ and the equilibrium spring lengths $\hat\zeta_{ij}$ (each arm’s $\tau$-node minus its study node) are read straight off the solution.

Raising $\tau^2$ softens every $\tau$-spring (drops every $\keff_{ij}$), letting the $\hat\zeta$ nodes slide out and down-weighting over-precise studies — the familiar random-effects shrinkage, now literal mechanical relaxation, exactly as the animation shows. The one missing ingredient is $\tau^2$ itself; the next page estimates it as twice the mean-square arm-spring length. For binomial observations, these stiffnesses describe each local IRLS step, as explained in 1b.
