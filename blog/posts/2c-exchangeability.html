Why the $\tau$-springs are exchangeable

Thodoris Papakonstantinou

The main animation · exchangeable arms, shared anchor
Arms are i.i.d. τ-springs on a shared anchor → exchangeable; the joint law is permutation-invariant.

Within-study contrast correlation

Sibling contrasts (arm−baseline) share the one anchor node, so each off-diagonal is exactly ½ and each diagonal 1 — the correlation matrix is (I+J)/2. Permute the arms: the labels move, the numbers do not. That invariance is exchangeability.

Inside one τ-chain: indistinguishable springs

A τ-chain is N elementary springs in series. Swap any two → the configuration is identical (bosons: no identity), so only N matters — which is why we count particles: Ξ = ΣN Z.
Orange spindle = the study's shared baseline node μ (the anchor every arm hangs from). Black coils = the four τ-springs, drawn identically because they are i.i.d. draws ζij ~ N(0, τ²/2). Blue coils = the observation springs to each treatment node A, B, C, D. Permuting arms swaps whole arms across rows; the anchor and the four identical τ-springs never change — that is why the model is exchangeable in its arms and why the sibling-contrast correlation is fixed at ½.

The four-arm study $S_3$ from the running network: four i.i.d. $\tau$-spring lengths $\zeta_{ij}$ on one shared study anchor. Press Permute arms — the joint law and the $(I+J)/2$ contrast correlation are unchanged. The inset shows two elementary springs in a chain swapping to an identical configuration.

meta-springs gives each arm one i.i.d. $\tau$-spring length, $\zeta_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2/2)$, all sharing a study’s baseline node. That single choice hands you the entire multi-arm covariance for free. A treatment contrast is a difference of arm lengths,

$$ \delta_{jk} = \zeta_{ij}-\zeta_{ik} \;\Longrightarrow\; \Var(\delta_{jk}) = \tau^2, \qquad \operatorname{corr}(\delta_{jk},\delta_{jl}) = \tfrac12, $$

the Lu–Ades $(I+J)/2$ structure — never written as a matrix, just inherited from the one shared anchor the two lengths are measured against. (This is exactly why we keep $\zeta$ for the arm-level lengths and $\delta$ for contrasts.)

The arm lengths $\zeta_{ij}$ are exchangeable, and that is precisely why the grand canonical picture is legitimate: the elementary springs making up a $\tau$-chain are indistinguishable bosons, so a configuration depends only on the chain length $N$, not on which springs. Exchangeability of the $\zeta$’s is what makes “count the particles” ($\Xi=\sum_N Z$) the right sum — the sum that Part 2 is built on.

Permuting the arms of a study (the animation’s Permute arms button) leaves the set of $\tau$-springs and the shared-anchor structure invariant — the contrast-correlation panel stays $(I+J)/2$ throughout. And swapping two elementary springs inside one $\tau$-chain gives an identical configuration: indistinguishability is what turns the sum over configurations into a sum over chain lengths alone.

FAQ
Is the per-arm (i.i.d.) random-effects parameterisation the same model as contrast-level Lu–Ades?

Yes, exactly — not an approximation. Each arm carries one length $\zeta_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2/2)$ sharing the study’s baseline node; a contrast is a difference $\delta_{jk}=\zeta_{ij}-\zeta_{ik}$, which reproduces the Lu–Ades $(I+J)/2$ covariance $\tau^2(I+J)/2$, with $\tau^2$ already on the conventional contrast scale. With equivalent baseline treatment and matching priors, the contrast likelihood and posterior agree. The full study-specific effect adds the mean contrast: $\theta_{i,ab}=(d_b-d_a)+(\zeta_{ib}-\zeta_{ia})$; see 1c.
