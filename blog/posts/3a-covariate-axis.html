A treatment effect may change with a study characteristic. Meta-regression asks how the effect changes with that covariate, and how much variation remains after accounting for it. The spring construction extends naturally: add slope coordinates to the equilibrium problem, then read their uncertainty from the same stiffness matrix.

There are two distinct cases. A supplied study covariate is a fixed input. A study’s latent baseline risk is an unknown parameter that must be fitted alongside the effects. Start with the fixed input: it makes the mechanics clear.

1. From an effect node to an effect line

Let $x_i^c=x_i-\bar x$. For continuous arm means, write

$$ y_{it}=\mu_i+d_t+\beta_{2,t} x_i^c+u_{it}+\epsilon_{it}, \qquad d_A=\beta_{2,A}=0, $$

with sampling variance $\Var(\epsilon_{it})=v_{it}$ and independent arm heterogeneity $\Var(u_{it})=\tau^2/2$. Here $\tau^2$ is on the contrast scale: subtracting two arms gives heterogeneity variance $\tau^2$.

Notation follows multinma (Phillippo et al. 2020): $\mu_i$ study baselines, $d_t$ treatment effects, $\beta_1$ prognostic coefficients and $\beta_{2,t}$ treatment–covariate interactions (multinma’s beta[x:.trtB] for treatment B). With one covariate value per study the prognostic term $\beta_1 x_i$ is constant within the study and is absorbed into $\mu_i$, so only $\beta_{2,t}$ appears here. Patient-level covariates, where $\beta_1$ becomes a tilted study rod, are in 3b (IPD) and 3c (IPD with aggregate data).

The treatment node becomes the line $d_t+\beta_{2,t} x^c$. Its intercept is the effect relative to A at the centring value; its slope is the change per unit of the covariate. The effect of C relative to B is therefore

$$ \Delta_{BC}(x)=(d_C-d_B)+(\beta_{2,C}-\beta_{2,B})(x-\bar x). $$

That subtraction matters. With a shared slope for all non-reference treatments, B versus A and C versus A both change with $x$, but C versus B does not: their slopes cancel. The reference treatment has slope zero.

  3. Select Unrelated slopes to let each rod tilt by its own slope.

The four-treatment network is the same illustrative network used earlier. Here its supplied $x_i$ values are $0.4,-0.8,0.6,1.2$. They are held fixed; this animation does not estimate latent baseline risk. The study rows are placed at the height of their covariate, so the treatment line $d_t+\beta_{2,t} x^c$ is drawn as a straight rod: its position at height $x$ is the effect at that covariate value, and its slope is the tilt. The rod is rigid but its displacement is not fixed: changing the slope by $\Delta$ moves the contact at height $x_i$ by $x_i^c\Delta$. So the horizontal gap between the untilted position (dashed line) and the rod at a study’s height is $\beta_{2,t} x_i^c$: the slope converts height into effect, and the covariate acts as a lever on the slope coordinate. A rod stores no energy, so the tilt does not introduce another freely varying residual.

2. The energy and the actual solve

Integrating the Gaussian arm deviations gives effective variance $v_{it}+\tau^2/2$. At fixed $\tau^2$, the energy is

$$ E(\theta)=\frac12\sum_{i,t} \frac{(y_{it}-\mu_i-d_t-\beta_{2,t} x_i^c)^2}{v_{it}+\tau^2/2} +\frac12\theta^T K\theta. $$

Here $\theta$ collects the baselines, effects and slopes. $K$ contains any Gaussian slope coupling. If $X$ contains the coefficients of those parameters and $W$ the inverse effective variances, force balance is

$$ A\hat\theta=b,\qquad A=X^T W X+K,\qquad b=X^T W y. $$

Each non-reference arm adds its covariate value to a slope column of $X$. This is precisely what Haskell’s nmaRegression and Rust’s nmr_fe example assemble. They store the study coordinate with the opposite sign ($\mu_i=-\phi_{S_i}$); the statistical model is the same. The covariate coefficients need not be $\pm1$, so these routines use a dense design-matrix solve rather than the ordinary edge-incidence Laplacian alone.

For this linear Gaussian model, at fixed variance components and with an identified design, the conditional posterior has mean $\hat\theta$ and covariance $A^{-1}$. The partition function includes both the energy at equilibrium and the fluctuation volume:

$$ \log Z(\tau^2)=-\frac12\left[ \sum_{i,t}\log(v_{it}+\tau^2/2)+\log\det A +y^T W y-b^T\hat\theta\right]+C. $$

$C$ collects terms independent of $\tau^2$ for this fixed model. For exchangeable slopes, also retain their variance-dependent prior normalisation. The determinant comes from integrating the effect and slope coordinates. This is the integrated Gaussian likelihood, rather than just the likelihood at its optimum.

The REML route iterates the heterogeneity estimate using residual stretches and their uncertainty. The grand canonical route evaluates a grid and weights its cells by $Z$, the variance prior and the integration measure. It gives a mixture of conditional Gaussians for the slopes as well as the effects. Gaussian integration within a cell is analytic; the finite grid is numerical. Residual heterogeneity need not decrease in every fitted dataset.

3. Sharing slopes is another spring construction

Structure Statistical model Mechanical interpretation
No covariate $\beta_{2,t}=0$ Remove the slope coordinates.
Shared $\beta_{2,t}=B$ for $t\ne A$ Use one slope coordinate.
Unrelated Separate $\beta_{2,t}$ Fit separate slope coordinates.
Exchangeable $\beta_{2,t}\sim N(B,\sigma_B^2)$ for $t\ne A$ Tie each slope to a common mean with stiffness $1/\sigma_B^2$.

Exchangeability adds the energy $\sum_{t\ne A}(\beta_{2,t}-B)^2/(2\sigma_B^2)$. Small $\sigma_B^2$ pulls the slopes together. Large $\sigma_B^2$ weakens that coupling. The GC calculation can integrate both residual heterogeneity $\tau^2$ and slope dispersion $\sigma_B^2$, connecting directly to multiple variance components. The animation currently shows the first three structures.

A slope needs covariate variation and a design that separates it from the treatment effects. Merely having different covariate values somewhere in the network does not guarantee that every treatment’s slope is identified.

4. When the covariate is itself unknown

For binary baseline-risk regression, the covariate is the latent reference-arm log-odds $\eta_i$. The implemented predictor is

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{it})=\eta_i+d_t+\beta_{2,t}(\eta_i-m_x), \qquad d_A=\beta_{2,A}=0. $$

$m_x$ is a fixed centring constant calculated from the reference arms. Unlike a supplied covariate, $\eta_i$ is inferred from the data. The product $\beta_{2,t}\eta_i$ makes this model nonlinear, even for the continuous-outcome version with an identity link.

Both implementations linearise the predictor using derivatives $1+\beta_{2,t}$, $1$, and $\eta_i-m_x$ with respect to the baseline, effect and slope. They solve the resulting weighted system and damp the step when needed. The baseline and slope therefore move together during fitting.

What the posterior calculation currently approximates

  • Gaussian outcomes, supplied covariate: analytic conditional integration followed by numerical variance-grid integration.
  • Binary outcomes, supplied covariate: Haskell’s nmaRegressionBinPosteriorB freezes a working Gaussian response at a shared-slope binomial fit, then integrates that approximation over the variance grid.
  • Latent baseline risk: the Haskell exchangeable GC routine and Rust’s brisk --method gc likewise freeze a local linearisation before the grid. Rust also applies a bilinear curvature correction, with a fallback when that corrected stiffness is not positive definite.

Thus these regression GC paths do not refit the nonlinear model at every grid cell. No MCMC does not mean an exact nonlinear posterior. In brisk, variance-grid weights are deterministic; the exported effect and slope draws are random draws from the resulting approximate mixture.

5. Run the model behind the picture

The solver is in the meta-springs repository; it needs Rust (cargo). Clone it, then build and run the Gaussian fixed-effect example on this page’s data:

git clone https://github.com/tpapak/meta-springs.git
cd meta-springs
cargo build --release --manifest-path gc-rust/Cargo.toml --example nmr_fe
gc-rust/target/release/examples/nmr_fe \
  blog/data/nmr-four-treatment.json blog/data/nmr-four-treatment-covariates.json

It prints:

beta = -1.08069
  A->B = 0.87176
  A->C = 1.56743
  A->D = 3.26861

The downloadable arm data and centred covariates reproduce the animation’s shared-slope fit at $\tau^2=0$. The command prints the slope and the effects relative to A. Covariates are centred in the input file: the example solver does not centre them automatically.

For the nonlinear baseline-risk model, use the separate bundled binary simulation, with A present in every study (from the same meta-springs directory):

cargo build --release --manifest-path gc-rust/Cargo.toml --bin brisk
gc-rust/target/release/brisk \
  --input test/nmr_br_sim_arms.json \
  --output /tmp/meta-springs-baseline-draws.json \
  --ref A --regressor common --effects random --method gc \
  --samples 4000 --seed 1

Change common to independent or exchangeable for the other slope structures. This CLI takes arm outcomes, not an external covariate file. Its current GC variance priors are half-normal with scale 0.5 on the heterogeneity SD and, when exchangeable, the slope SD; they are hard-coded.

The output includes mx, treatment order, d_draws, b_draws, and tau2 (the GC mean of the contrast-scale variance). Paired effect and slope draws let you evaluate $d_t+\beta_{2,t}(\eta-m_x)$ at a specified baseline log-odds $\eta$. The d_hat and b_hat fields are the initial fitted point estimates, not the GC mixture means. The baseline-risk example uses a different dataset because the four-study animation includes a C–D study without reference A.

FAQ
Priors on the basic parameters d, not just on τ?

A prior on a $d$ (or on $\beta_{2,t}$ in meta-regression) is a spring to ground: a spring from that node to its prior mean with stiffness = prior precision (Gaussian prior). Non-Gaussian priors are non-linear springs, handled by the same Newton relaxation. Flat priors = no ground spring.
