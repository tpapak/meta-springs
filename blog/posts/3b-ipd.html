In 3a a covariate was a property of a study: one value per trial, and the slope was learned from differences between trials. Individual participant data (IPD) record the covariate and the outcome for every patient. The spring network takes them as they are: one spring per patient.

1. A patient is a spring

Patient $i$ in study $j$ on treatment $k$ has linear predictor $\eta_i=\mu_j+d_k$ (covariates come in section 3). As in Part 1, the spring runs from the study node to the treatment node, and its energy is the patient’s negative log-likelihood:

  • Continuous outcome, $y_i\sim\mathcal N(\eta_i,\sigma^2)$: $E_i=(y_i-\eta_i)^2/2\sigma^2$. A Hooke spring with rest length $y_i$ and stiffness $1/\sigma^2$.
  • Binary outcome, $y_i\in\{0,1\}$, $\operatorname{logit}p_i=\eta_i$: $E_i=-y_i\log p_i-(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)$. The force is $y_i-p_i$ and the stiffness $p_i(1-p_i)$: the binomial spring of 1b with $n=1$. A single patient has no rest length: a responder pushes $\eta$ up, a non-responder pushes it down, and neither push ever reaches zero.

2. Without covariates, patients add up to the arm spring

If every patient in an arm has the same $\eta$, their springs act in parallel and add:

$$ \sum_{i\in\text{arm}}\frac{(y_i-\eta)^2}{2\sigma^2} =\frac{n(\bar y-\eta)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\text{const},\qquad \sum_{i\in\text{arm}}(y_i-p)=r-np. $$

The $n$ Gaussian patients are one spring of stiffness $n/\sigma^2$ at the arm mean $\bar y$: the arm spring of 1a with $v=\sigma^2/n$. The $n$ binary patients pull exactly like the arm’s binomial spring with $r$ events. The arm totals are sufficient statistics, so IPD and arm totals give the same equilibrium. In the animation, select Summed into arm springs: the panel compares the two fits, and they differ only by rounding ($10^{-15}$).

So IPD changes nothing until patients differ in something that matters for their outcome.

3. Patient covariates give the patients heights

With a patient covariate $x$, in multinma’s notation,

$$ \eta_i=\mu_j+\beta_1(x_i-\bar x)+d_k+\beta_{2,k}(x_i-\bar x), \qquad d_A=\beta_{2,A}=0. $$

Put each patient at the height of their own $x$:

  • Study rods. The study node of 3a opens into a rod, tilted by the prognostic slope $\beta_1$. Each study keeps its own position $\mu_j$; all study rods share the tilt.
  • Treatment rods. As in 3a, treatment $k$ is a rod at $d_k$ tilted by the interaction $\beta_{2,k}$, glued through every study that has it. The reference $A$ is pinned and vertical. The animation shares one interaction between B and C, $\beta_{2,B}=\beta_{2,C}=\beta_2$.
  • Springs. Patient $i$ is a spring from the study rod to their treatment rod at height $x_i$. Its horizontal length is $\eta_i$.

Now the patients of one arm sit at different heights, see different $\eta$, and no longer collapse into one spring.

4. Levers: the within-study spread identifies the slopes

Tilting a rod by $\Delta$ moves its contact at height $x$ by $(x-\bar x)\Delta$. A spring pulling with force $F_i$ at height $x_i$ turns the rod with torque $(x_i-\bar x)F_i$, and resists the tilt with stiffness $(x_i-\bar x)^2k_i$. At equilibrium

$$ \sum_i (x_i-\bar x)\,F_i=0, $$

which is the score equation for the slope, and $\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2k_i$ is its Fisher information. The heights are the lever arms:

  • A patient at the pivot $\bar x$ says nothing about a slope. Patients far from it say the most.
  • The slope’s standard error falls in proportion to the covariate’s spread within the studies. Halve the spread and the standard error roughly doubles.
  • Summing an arm into one spring puts all its patients at one height: no lever, no slope. That is why the Summed into arm springs view switches the covariate terms off.

This is the main gain of IPD over the study-level regression of 3a. There the slope came from comparing trials with different average $x$, which is confounded with anything else that differs between the trials (ecological or aggregation bias). Here each study rod has its own position $\mu_j$, so between-study differences in baseline are absorbed; $\beta_2$ is learned by comparing randomised arms at the same $x$. In this two-study network each active treatment appears in only one study, so $d_B$ and $d_C$ also absorb any between-study difference in effect and $\beta_2$ comes from within-trial comparisons alone. In larger networks a one-stage model can mix within- and between-study information on an interaction unless the covariate is centred within each study (Riley et al. 2020, Statistics in Medicine).

Try the picture

  1. Start with Binary outcome and $\beta_1$ + interaction $\beta_2$. The estimates sit near the simulation truth; note the standard errors of $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$.
  2. Select Summed into arm springs. Each arm becomes one spring labelled with its totals, the rods stand vertical, and the panel shows that the patient fit and the arm fit without covariates are the same.
  3. Back to Patients as springs. Drag the within-study covariate spread down to $\times 0.25$: the patients bunch up around each study’s mean, the levers shorten, and the slope standard errors grow three to four times. Drag it up to $\times 2$ and they fall, but by less than half: patients at extreme heights have $p$ near 0 or 1, and their springs go soft (stiffness $p(1-p)$).
  4. Switch to Continuous outcome. Each patient is now a Hooke spring whose rest length is their outcome (the dot). Orange springs are compressed and push the rods apart; blue ones are stretched and pull them together. The standard errors are much smaller: a continuous outcome with residual SD 0.5 carries more information per patient than a yes/no answer. Here every spring has the same stiffness, so the slope standard errors are exactly inversely proportional to the spread.

5. The equilibrium is the glm() fit

With no priors, the equilibrium is the maximum-likelihood estimate and the stiffness matrix at equilibrium is the observed information. For the logit link (the canonical link) the observed and expected information coincide, so the spring network reproduces glm() exactly, standard errors included; for a continuous outcome it reproduces lm(). The animation solves the network by Newton’s method in the browser, and its panel matches R to every digit shown.

The patients in the animation (spread $\times 1$) are in ipd-two-studies.csv. With the springs R package from the meta-springs repository and multinma installed:

library(multinma)
d <- read.csv("ipd-two-studies.csv")
d$xc <- d$x - 0.5                                        # centre at x-bar = 0.5
d$trtclass <- ifelse(d$trt == "A", "control", "active")  # one interaction for B and C
net <- set_ipd(d, study = study, trt = trt, r = y_binary, trt_class = trtclass)
fit <- springs::mlnmr(net, regression = ~ xc + xc:.trt, class_interactions = "common",
                      link = "logit", likelihood = "bernoulli", center = FALSE,
                      prior_intercept = Inf, prior_trt = Inf, prior_reg = Inf)

d$act <- as.numeric(d$trt != "A")
g <- glm(y_binary ~ 0 + study + trt + xc + xc:act, family = binomial, data = d)
cbind(springs = fit$mode, glm = coef(g))

The largest difference is $7\times10^{-8}$, the solver’s convergence tolerance. On real data, the three UNCOVER psoriasis trials in multinma (26 parameters, five covariates and their interactions with treatment) give the same agreement: identical to glm() for the logit link and within $5\times10^{-8}$ for the probit.

The prior_* arguments set to Inf mean flat priors. Finite values add springs to ground, and springs::mlnmr then returns the posterior rather than the maximum-likelihood point, without MCMC.

Usually only some trials share their patients. In 3c Study 2 keeps only its arm totals and its covariate mean and SD, and its arms become springs averaged over that distribution.

FAQ
Is this one-stage or two-stage IPD meta-analysis?

One-stage: every patient’s spring is in one network, solved once. A two-stage analysis would first fit each trial and then pool the trial estimates as contrast springs. Without covariates and with Gaussian outcomes the two give the same treatment effects (section 2). With covariates or binary outcomes they can differ, and the one-stage network keeps the exact patient likelihood.

Do I need IPD from every trial?

No. Trials without IPD enter through their published arm totals and covariate summaries; that is multilevel network meta-regression, in 3c.
