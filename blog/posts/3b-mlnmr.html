ML-NMR: patient covariates, study rods and averaged springs

In 3a every study had one covariate value. Its arms all sat at one height, so the prognostic term was absorbed into the study baseline and only the treatment slopes appeared. Multilevel network meta-regression (ML-NMR; Phillippo et al. 2020, implemented in multinma) uses covariates measured on patients: individual participant data (IPD) from some trials, and published covariate summaries from the others. The change of picture is one step: give every study row a height.

1. The model in multinma’s notation

For a patient in study $j$ on treatment $k$ with covariate $x$,

$$ \operatorname{logit} p = \mu_j + \beta_1 (x-\bar x) + d_k + \beta_{2,k}(x-\bar x), \qquad d_A=\beta_{2,A}=0. $$

Symbol Meaning multinma output
$\mu_j$ study baseline mu[j]
$d_k$ treatment effect at $\bar x$ d[k]
$\beta_1$ prognostic slope: changes response on every arm beta[x]
$\beta_{2,k}$ interaction: changes the treatment effect beta[x:.trtk]

The animation shares one interaction across the active treatments, $\beta_{2,B}=\beta_{2,C}=\beta_2$.

2. Study rods appear

Put each patient at the height of their own $x$.

  • Treatment rods. As in 3a, treatment $k$ is a rigid rod at $d_k$, tilted by $\beta_{2,k}$ about the pivot $\bar x$. It is glued through every study: all studies see the same position and tilt (consistency). The reference $A$ is pinned and vertical.
  • Study rods. The study spindle of 3a opens into a rod over the study’s covariate range, tilted by $\beta_1$. Study rods are glued in tilt only: each keeps its own baseline $\mu_j$, but all tilt by the same $\beta_1$, so they stay parallel.
  • Springs. A patient is a spring from the study rod to the treatment rod at the patient’s height. Its horizontal length is the patient’s log-odds $\eta$ (the study rod sits at $-\mu_j$, as in 1b).

This is why 3a had no study rods: with one $x$ per study the rod has no length, and tilting it is the same as moving $\mu_j$. $\beta_1$ is identified only when $x$ varies within a study.

3. What the tilt does: levers

The rods are rigid but their displacement is not fixed. Tilting a rod by $\Delta$ moves its contact at height $x$ by $(x-\bar x)\Delta$. The rod stores no energy; it transmits force:

  • A spring pulling with force $F_i$ turns the tilt with force $(x_i-\bar x)F_i$ and resists it with stiffness $(x_i-\bar x)^2 k_i$.
  • The balance $\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)F_i=0$ is the score equation for $\beta_1$ (all arms) or $\beta_2$ (active arms); $\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2k_i$ is its Fisher information.
  • A patient at $\bar x$ sits at the pivot and says nothing about a slope. If every patient sat at one height, tilt and position could not be separated. The heights are the entries of the design matrix.

The link function changes the springs, not the rods, so the same rods serve Gaussian, binomial and survival likelihoods.

4. Binary patients have no rest length

A single Bernoulli observation has no finite best value of $\eta$: a responder pushes $\eta$ up with force $1-p$, a non-responder pushes it down with force $p$ (1b with $n=1$). In the animation responders are green and non-responders grey. The equilibrium exists because the two push against each other through the rods. Without covariates every patient in an arm shares $\eta$, the pushes balance at $\operatorname{logit}(r/n)$, and the patients add up exactly to the arm’s binomial spring. With covariates they sit at different heights and stay separate.

If all responders sat at one end of the covariate range and all non-responders at the other, every levered push would turn the tilt the same way and the slope would run off to infinity: complete separation. A prior on the slope (a spring to ground) stops it.

5. Aggregate arms: one spring with extent

Study 2 reports only arm totals $r$ of $n$ and the covariate mean and SD. Its arm is still one spring, but the spring has extent: it sits at every height of the arm’s covariate distribution. Draw integration points $x_q$ from that distribution; average the probability, then form one binomial energy:

$$ \eta_q=\mu_j+\beta_1(x_q-\bar x)+d_k+\beta_{2,k}(x_q-\bar x),\qquad \bar p=\frac1Q\sum_q \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta_q),\qquad E=-\log\operatorname{Bin}(r\mid n,\bar p). $$

Replacing the sheath by one spring at the mean height, $\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\bar\eta)$, is the at-mean-covariate model. The difference is the aggregation bias, to second order

$$ \bar p-\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\bar\eta)\approx \tfrac12\,\sigma''(\bar\eta)\,(\beta_1+\beta_{2,k})^2\operatorname{Var}(x). $$

It vanishes with no covariate spread, with an identity link, and at $p=1/2$; it grows toward the tails. The averaged arm also feels the tilt through all its heights: its effective lever is the $\sigma'$-weighted mean of $x_q$, not the plain mean.

  2. Switch to at mean covariate. $d_C$ shifts, and the anchored C vs B comparison changes sign. The panel also shows, at the current fit, the C arm’s averaged response next to its response at the mean covariate.
  3. Move the reported SD to 0: the sheath collapses to one spring and the two models agree. Increase it and they separate.

6. What is implemented

The engine (gc-rust/src/ipd.rs, gc-rust/src/bin/mlnmr.rs) builds these springs, finds the equilibrium by Newton’s method on the exact energy, and corrects the Gaussian curvature by importance weights against the exact likelihood. The R wrapper takes a multinma network:

library(multinma)   # set_ipd(), set_agd_arm(), combine_network(), add_integration()
fit <- springs::mlnmr(net,
         regression = ~(durnpso + prevsys + bsa + weight + psa) * .trt,
         class_interactions = "common", link = "probit", likelihood = "bernoulli2")

On the plaque psoriasis network (four IPD trials with 4,114 patients, five aggregate trials) it reproduces multinma’s posteriors to within 0.12 posterior standard deviations, in 1–4 seconds instead of 33–65.

FAQ
Why can’t the at-mean model just be fitted by a standard GLM tool?

It can, and that is the problem: it has one linear predictor per observation, the form INLA and ordinary GLM software require. The averaged spring depends on a non-linear average over many linear predictors, which those tools cannot express. That average is exactly where aggregation bias is removed.

Where does $\beta_1$ go when only aggregate data are available?

With only aggregate studies, $\beta_1$ is informed only through the averaging across different covariate distributions, which is weak. IPD from at least some studies is what pins the slopes; the aggregate studies then borrow them.
